\begin{abstract}
Land-use regulation in cities depends not only on the formal rules, but also on the individual politicians who administer them.
Many U.S. cities allow substantial informal discretion in the development approval process, often exercised by the local politician representing the district where development is proposed.
I study informal regulatory discretion in Chicago, where ``aldermanic privilege'' gives city council members substantial influence over development in their wards.
I construct alderman-level ``stringency scores'' from permit processing times and use complementary event-study and boundary-discontinuity designs to estimate the effects of these informal regulations on housing supply and prices.
Assignment to a more stringent alderman lowers permitting activity by 12\%, while multifamily density is 13--17\% lower immediately on the more-stringent side of a ward boundary.
I also find that home prices are 3.0\% higher on the more-stringent side, while rents are 1.8\% higher but not statistically distinguishable from zero.
These results show that informal regulation can distort housing supply and potentially affect citywide housing affordability, even in areas where the formal zoning rules are similar.
\end{abstract}
