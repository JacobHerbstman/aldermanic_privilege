\begin{abstract}
Land use regulation constrains housing supply, but formal zoning rules are only part of the regulatory environment developers face.
I study informal regulatory discretion in Chicago, where ``aldermanic privilege'' allows city council members to block, delay, or reshape development in their wards.
To study this phenomenon, I construct alderman-level ``stringency scores'' from permit processing times to proxy for the regulatory environment in each ward.
I then exploit ward redistricting in 2015 and find that census blocks reassigned toward more stringent aldermen reduces the number of permits issued by about 12\%.
I then turn to a complementary boundary-discontinuity design to examine the downstream effects of aldermanic discretion on new construction density and prices.
At ward boundaries, multifamily projects have about 14\% lower floor-area ratio (FAR) and 18\% fewer dwelling units per acre (DUPAC) immediately across the boundary on the more-stringent side.
There also is a large effect on home prices, which are on average about 4.6\% higher, and an imprecise jump of about 1.7\% in rents on the more-stringent side of ward boundaries.
These findings show that informal political discretion can constrain housing supply even when the formal regulatory environment is similar.
\end{abstract}
