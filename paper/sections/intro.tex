\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Housing costs in the United States have risen sharply, and a large literature points to land-use regulation as a central cause.
Restrictive zoning, permitting frictions, and inclusionary zoning requirements limit housing supply, raise prices, and distort the allocation of labor across cities \citep{glaeser_why_2005, saiz_geographic_2010, turner_land_2014, hsieh_housing_2019, duranton_urban_2023, soltas_price_2024, soltas_how_2026}.
However, formal zoning rules are not the only determinant of what gets built.
Informal regulations, often exercised through the discretionary power of local politicians, also play a significant role in shaping development patterns.
Many major cities in the United States have a fragmented regulatory approval system that includes the discretionary judgment of the local city councilmember or a similar politician.\footnote{Analogous forms of council-member deference in land-use decisions have been documented in Philadelphia, Los Angeles, and New York City \citep{burnett_local_2014, jacobson_dysfunctional_2023, mitre-becerril_does_2024, new_york_city_charter_revision_commission_charter_2025}.}
This potential for individual discretion to differentiate otherwise similar regulatory environments can induce uncertainty in the development process and potentially lower housing supply in cities \citep{mayer_land_2000, hilber_origins_2013, khan_decentralized_2021, mast_warding_2024}.
Informal discretion from individual politicians can substantially lengthen the development timeline.
When developers face long and unpredictable timelines, projects shrink, marginal investments become unviable, and housing supply falls \citep{cunningham_house_2006, bloom_impact_2009, oh_land_2024}.
Despite this evidence, there is little empirical work on how individual politicians use these informal regulatory powers to shape housing supply.

This paper studies this form of informal regulatory power in the context of Chicago's ``aldermanic privilege,'' the unwritten but influential authority of each of Chicago's 50 city council members (aldermen) to unilaterally block or approve development in that member's ward.
Chicago has no city charter, so there is no statutory basis for this power \citep{mcclintock_what_2025}.
It operates through an unspoken mutual understanding: other aldermen vote with the local member on land use matters in exchange for similar support in their own wards, city agencies follow the local alderman's lead, and developers understand that major projects require the alderman's blessing \citep{thale_aldermanic_2005, einstein_neighborhood_2019, khan_decentralized_2021}.
This creates sharp changes in regulatory authority at ward boundaries, even when the neighborhood and zoning are quite similar.
Two parcels on opposite sides of the same street, in the same neighborhood, with the same zoning, can face very different regulatory regimes depending on which alderman represents them.
I use this sudden change in regulatory authority to study how informal regulations affect housing supply and prices.

To measure the regulatory environment each alderman creates, I construct ``regulatory stringency scores'' from processing times for permits likely to involve substantial aldermanic discretion, which include new construction, renovations, and demolitions.
I deem these ``high-discretion'' permits since they are the most likely to require aldermanic approval.
The motivation for using processing time variation is simple: delay makes projects less valuable, and
a developer who expects years of meetings, redesigns, and uncertainty may build a smaller project or be deterred from building at all \citep{kydland_time_1982, mayer_land_2000, paciorek_supply_2013, gyourko_local_2021, soltas_how_2026}.

I first use the 2015 ward redistricting to test whether permitting changes when a census block is reassigned to a different ward.
This design holds location fixed while changing the assigned alderman.
Using this within-census-block variation,
I find that reassignment toward greater stringency reduces the annual number of high-discretion permits recorded by the city by about 12\% during 2015--2020.
This result is driven asymmetrically by movement toward more stringent aldermen: these census blocks see a 20.6\% reduction in high-discretion permits, compared with a 6.9\% increase for blocks reassigned to a more lenient alderman.
I find no statistically significant effect on low-discretion permits, consistent with an effect concentrated in permits more exposed to aldermanic discretion.

I then examine whether new housing is built at lower density under more stringent aldermen.
Following \citet{bayer_unified_2007} and \citet{kulka_under_2026}, I employ nonparametric regression-discontinuity designs at ward boundaries to estimate effects on new construction density.
I find a 9\% reduction in FAR and a 14\% reduction in DUPAC on the more-stringent side of ward boundaries, with slightly larger effects for multifamily new construction.

I also compare rents and sale prices across ward boundaries.
I find a sharp increase in home prices across the boundary of about 3.0\%.
I also find a 1.8\% increase in listed rents, but the estimate is imprecise and not statistically significant.
Although these results are more suggestive, their direction is consistent with lower supply on the more-stringent side and with informal regulation affecting affordability.

This paper contributes most directly to work on local political control and fragmented land-use authority.
\citet{fischel_homevoter_2009} argues that homeowners use local institutions to restrict development and protect property values, and subsequent work shows how decentralized jurisdictions can adopt restrictions that are locally rational but collectively costly \citep{hilber_origins_2013, ortalo-magne_political_2014, monarrez_dividing_2023, mast_warding_2024, bordeu_commuting_2025, kulka_under_2026}.
I show that politicians within a fragmented system can create different regulatory environments even when formal rules are shared.

In Chicago specifically, \citet{freemark_upzoning_2020} studies formal upzoning and \citet{khan_decentralized_2021} finds that aldermen approve smaller rezonings near ward boundaries where the benefits to their constituents are lower.
I add to this literature by showing that the effects of informal regulatory discretion extend beyond formal zoning or statutory upzonings and appear in permitting activity and the density of new construction, not only in zoned density.

The paper also contributes to work on permitting delays and time-to-build, since the effects of these informal regulations are often realized through development uncertainty and delays.
Development lags are a first-order determinant of housing supply \citep{mayer_land_2000, mayer_residential_2000, paciorek_supply_2013}.
Recent work finds that rules-based planning shortens construction timelines and that developers place substantial value on lots with ready-to-issue permits \citep{lyons_time_2025, soltas_how_2026}.
I use alderman-specific variation in permitting time and connect this to both permit activity and completed housing density.
