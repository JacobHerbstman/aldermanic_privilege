\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies how informal regulations and the discretion of individual political actors affect housing supply within a city.
I focus on Chicago's aldermanic privilege, the informal power of each of the city's 50 aldermen to block or approve development in that member's ward.
This institution allows individual politicians to create different regulatory environments within a single municipal system.

Using permit processing times for projects where aldermen are most likely to matter, I construct aldermanic stringency scores that isolate the alderman-specific component of regulatory delay after accounting for permit, location, time, and ward characteristics.
Using the 2015 ward redistricting to identify changes in assigned stringency, I find that reassignment toward greater stringency reduces the annual number of high-discretion permits recorded by the city by about 12\% in the five years following the remap.
A boundary comparison shows that new construction is less dense on the more-stringent side of ward boundaries, with multifamily FAR about 14\% lower and DUPAC about 18\% lower immediately across the boundary.
I also find a 2.9\% increase in home prices and a suggestive 1.7\% increase in listed rents, although the rent estimate is imprecise.

These results show that formal zoning rules do not fully describe the regulatory environment facing a developer.
Similar projects governed by the same municipal and zoning codes can face different timelines and prospects for approval depending on the local politician.
Effective land-use reforms must address not only the formal rules governing what can be built, but also the informal political institutions that determine whether and how development proceeds.
