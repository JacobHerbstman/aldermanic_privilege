\section{Results}
\label{sec:empirical_results}

\subsection{Permit Activity After Redistricting}
\label{sec:empirical_results_permits}

I first ask whether permit activity changes when the same location is assigned to a different alderman.
Chicago's 2015 ward redistricting moved some census blocks toward wards represented by more stringent aldermen and others toward wards represented by more lenient aldermen.
Nearby blocks that remained in their original ward provide the comparison group.
The design therefore holds the physical location fixed while changing its assigned regulatory environment.
The primary analysis sample keeps all boundaries where the aldermen representing both the origin and destination wards in 2014 remained in office when the new map took effect,
since newly elected aldermen could have preferences toward development that are endogenous to the previous development activity in the ward.
I also require each block to have at least one high-discretion permit during the five years before redistricting.
Without that restriction, any later permit on a block with no previous activity would mechanically appear as an increase.

The outcome is the number of high-discretion permits recorded on each census block in each year from 2010 through 2020.
The outcome therefore measures when projects observed in the permit data first appeared in the process.
These are the high-discretion permits defined in Section~\ref{subsec:permit_pipeline}.

I estimate a Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood event study that treats moves toward greater stringency and greater leniency as opposite changes:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event_study_permits}
\begin{aligned}
\log E[Y_{bt}] ={}&
\alpha_b + \lambda_{gt}
+ \sum_{\tau \neq -1}
\beta_\tau Z_b \mathbf{1}\{r_{bt}=\tau\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
Here $Y_{bt}$ is the permit count for block $b$ in year $t$, $\alpha_b$ is a block fixed effect, and $\lambda_{gt}$ is a border-pair-by-year fixed effect.
$Z_b$ equals one when the new map assigned block $b$ toward a more stringent alderman, minus one when it assigned the block toward a more lenient alderman, and zero when the block remained in the same ward.
I classify moves using the 2014 incumbents in each block's origin and destination wards, with scores estimated only from permits filed between 2006 and 2014.
I do not redefine treatment using the 2015 election winners because those elections may themselves reflect local preferences toward development.
This specification imposes equal-and-opposite effects in logs for assignments toward more stringent and more lenient aldermen.
Panels B and C relax that restriction by estimating the two directions separately.
\onlineappendixlink{Table C.2}{tab:permit_event_study_did} reports the corresponding pooled estimates without this restriction and shows the results are nearly identical.
An interesting heterogeneity appears when the two directions are estimated separately: the decline in permitting activity mostly comes from movements toward more stringent aldermen,
who see a 23\% reduction in high-discretion permits, while blocks assigned toward more lenient aldermen see a 6.5\% increase.
So it appears that aldermanic discretion comes from uniquely stringent aldermen blocking activity that would otherwise have occurred, rather than uniquely lenient aldermen allowing activity that would generally be blocked under formal city regulations.
This difference is crucial for determining the likely aggregate effect of this informal regulatory discretion on housing supply, since it is ex-ante possible that informal discretion allows a few particularly pro-development aldermen to approve more housing than a centralized system.
My results suggest that this is not the case and it appears that this system of informal regulations likely reduces housing supply relative to a more centralized counterfactual.


Panel A of Figure~\ref{fig:permit_event_study} plots the coefficients $\beta_\tau$.
The pre-period coefficients show no systematic divergence, and the pre-trend test has a $p$-value of 0.394.
After redistricting, the estimate becomes negative.
The pooled estimate is $-0.127$ (SE $=0.049$), equivalent to an 11.9\% reduction in the annual number of high-discretion permits associated with a move toward greater stringency.
Panels B and C show that the decline comes from blocks assigned toward more stringent aldermen rather than a corresponding increase among blocks assigned toward more lenient aldermen.
This decline in permit activity complements the building-density evidence below.

\subsection{Density at Ward Boundaries}
\label{sec:empirical_results_density}

I next examine completed new construction on opposite sides of the same ward boundary.
Projects within 500ft of the same boundary segment face similar neighborhood amenities and demand conditions, but different aldermen.
Following \citet{bayer_unified_2007} and \citet{kulka_under_2026}, I divide signed distance to the boundary into 100ft bands and compare projects in each band with projects in the nearest band on the less-stringent side.
This flexible specification does not require density to follow a linear or polynomial relationship with distance.
I estimate
%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:segment_fe}
\begin{aligned}
    \ln(Y_{ist}) ={}&
    \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B},\,b \neq [-100,0)}
    \tau_b \mathbf{1}\{d_{is} \in b\}
    + X_i'\theta \\
    &\quad
    + \alpha_{z(i,t)}
    + \delta_s
    + \gamma_t
    + \varepsilon_{ist}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
Here $Y_{ist}$ is FAR or DUPAC for project $i$ on boundary segment $s$, built in year $t$.
The running variable $d_{is}$ is negative on the less-stringent side and positive on the more-stringent side.
The set $\mathcal{B}$ contains ten 100ft bands from $-500$ to 500ft.
The band from $-100$ to 0ft is omitted, so $\tau_{[0,100)}$ compares projects immediately across the boundary.
The vector $X_i$ contains share White, share Black, median household income, bachelor's share, and homeownership on the project's side of the boundary.
The model also includes the zoning group in force when the project was built, boundary-segment fixed effects, and construction-year fixed effects.
Standard errors are clustered by ward pair.

Figure~\ref{fig:rd_fe_plots} plots the distance-band estimates.
The omitted band is normalized to zero, and the subtitle in each panel reports $\tau_{[0,100)}$.
For all new construction, FAR is about 12\% lower and dwelling units per acre about 16\% lower immediately across the boundary on the more-stringent side.
Among multifamily projects, FAR is about 14\% lower and dwelling units per acre about 18\% lower.
All four differences are statistically distinguishable from zero at the 10\% level or better.
The similar results for FAR and dwelling units per acre indicate that the difference is present in both floor space and the number of homes placed on a parcel.

As a falsification test, I repeat the same comparison at artificial cutoffs 1000ft inside either side of the true ward boundary.
These locations do not mark a change in aldermanic authority.
\onlineappendixlink{Figure D.1}{fig:rd_placebo_shifts} shows no corresponding break: none of the eight nearest-band placebo estimates is statistically distinguishable from zero.
The contrast with the true boundary makes it less likely that the main estimates reflect a general spatial pattern within wards.
\onlineappendixlink{D}{sec:appendix_density} presents the placebo figures along with continuity tests for fixed location characteristics, restrictions based on boundary geometry, and donut checks.

\subsection{Evidence on Rents and Sale Prices}
\label{sec:empirical_results_prices}

The permit and density results indicate that more stringent aldermen reduce development activity and the density of completed housing.
The model in \ref{sec:model}, indicates that this should feed into prices, which is the most directly policy-relevant outcome given Chicago's current rent and price growth.
For rents and sales, I estimate
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:price_rd}
    \log(Y_{ist}) =
    \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}}
    \tau_b \mathbf{1}\{d_{is} \in b\}
    + X_i'\gamma
    + \alpha_{st}
    + \varepsilon_{ist},
\end{equation}
%
where $Y_{ist}$ is the real listed rent or real sale price for observation $i$ near boundary segment $s$ in period $t$.
As in the density analysis, $\mathcal{B}$ contains ten 100ft distance bands and the band from $-100$ to 0ft is omitted.
The coefficient $\tau_{[0,100)}$ therefore compares observations in the two bands immediately across the boundary.
The fixed effects compare observations near the same boundary segment in the same month for rents and the same quarter for sales.
The controls include unit or property characteristics and distances to nearby schools, parks, major roads, CTA stops, and Lake Michigan.
Standard errors are clustered by ward pair.

Figure~\ref{fig:price_boundary_bins} plots these distance-band estimates.

Listed rents are 1.7\% higher in the two bands closest to the boundary, although the estimate is imprecise.
Home sale prices are 4.6\% higher in the two bands closest to the boundary.
I treat the sales result as suggestive because transactions are less frequent near any particular boundary and their composition is harder to hold fixed.
\onlineappendixlink{E}{sec:appendix_price_rd} shows that the result disappears at artificial cutoffs inside either ward and is similar when I restrict the sample to straight boundaries or exclude observations immediately next to the boundary.
