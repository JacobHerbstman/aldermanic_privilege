\section{Institutional Background and Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Aldermanic Privilege and the Development Process}
\label{subsec:aldermanic_privilege}

Chicago's City Council consists of 50 aldermen, each representing a single ward.
Chicago's uniquely informal norms give each alderman broad authority over
development within ward boundaries.
This practice, known as ``aldermanic
privilege'' or ``aldermanic prerogative,'' rests on custom rather than formal law, in part because Chicago has no city charter \citep{mcclintock_what_2025}.
On matters affecting a
single ward, other aldermen defer to the local representative, and city agencies
follow the alderman's lead \citep{thale_aldermanic_2005, zhang_boundaries_2011}.
The convention is strong
enough that aldermen have often been described as ``little mayors'' governing
their wards as personal fiefdoms \citep{einhorn_property_1991}.
One alderman
described the system candidly: ``I often liken the City of Chicago to a feudal
system, where the mayor is sort of a de facto king.
And each alderman is the lord
of their individual fiefdom.''
\citep{keefe_pregnancy_2013}

\paragraph{The formal mechanism.}
Two reforms narrowed this authority in 2019.
A new construction code repealed a rule that had generally required the Department of Buildings to notify the local alderman of permit applications and wait ten days.
Mayor Lightfoot's Executive Order 2019-2 separately directed city departments not to defer to aldermen when municipal law did not require it.
Neither reform eliminated aldermanic influence: other notification requirements remained, departments continued to solicit aldermanic input, and aldermen retained City Council authority over zoning and other legislative approvals \citep{city_of_chicago_sixty-day_2019, mayors_press_office_mayor_2019, schutz_lightfoot_2019}.
Lightfoot captured the problem directly: ``If
you've got 50 separate fiefdoms that you have to deal with on issues like affordable
housing, or just getting a sign on your building, it makes doing business with the
city of Chicago damn near impossible.''
\citep{spielman_lightfoot_2019} The Metropolitan Planning Council made the same point from the developer side: ``before a development even gets to the point of being indefinitely
delayed, many never make it past `Go' because of required aldermanic letters of
support.''
\citep{metropolitan_planning_council_unfettered_2019}

\paragraph{How prerogative shapes development.}
Aldermanic
discretion enters well before formal permit issuance and continues after a project is filed.

\textit{Pre-application.}
Experienced Chicago developers know that projects requiring rezoning or smaller
modifications need the alderman's support before formal papers are filed.
Although not legally required, aldermanic support is all but essential in practice: without it, a
developer risks investing heavily in design, legal, and engineering work
before facing rejection at the political stage.
One anonymous Chicago architect
described the dynamic: ``To get the alderman, you have to get the neighborhood
group, and that's where the trouble comes in.
It's fraught with risk, even to have
this conversation.''
\citep{giangreco_game_2024}

\textit{Rezoning.}
For projects requiring a change to the zoning code, the
alderman's role is effectively dispositive.
Although zoning amendments formally
require a City Council vote, the council ``routinely shows unquestioning deference
to local aldermen even in the absence of any articulated local concern''
\citep{chase_chicago_2023}.
Equally consequential is the alderman's ability to
preemptively \textit{downzone} parcels to foreclose future development.
Joseph
Schwieterman, a DePaul University professor who studies Chicago land use, noted
that the practice ``creates real uncertainty for real estate developers when
downzoning becomes ad hoc or driven by the desire of an alderman to settle scores.''
\citep{gallun_ad_2019} Documented episodes include Ald.\ Brendan Reilly's
proposals to downzone a River North site where a developer had proposed a 60-story
tower and a separate site to block a 615-room hotel, and a Pilsen downzoning
orchestrated by Ald.\ Solis that stripped a parcel of its residential designation
to kill a planned 465-unit apartment project.

\textit{Permit processing.}
For projects that proceed by right under existing
zoning, the alderman retains substantial informal influence over the Department of
Buildings permit queue.
The corruption case against Ald.\ Ed Burke illustrated the
mechanism in an extreme form: federal prosecutors documented how his staff
withheld routine permits--including a driveway permit for a fast-food
restaurant--until the property owner directed legal business to Burke's law firm
\citep{spielman_aldermen_2019}.
The case made visible what developers had long
understood: in Chicago, permit timing is another lever aldermen can use to delay and suppress
projects they oppose.
In interviews, a former alderman described substantial use of the permit process to deter developers from trying to overrule the alderman on rezoning and planning matters earlier in the process.

\paragraph{A recent example.}
Developer Fern Hill began community
outreach in 2021, proposing a roughly 500-unit mixed-income tower at a
transit-accessible site in the 2nd Ward, with approximately 100 affordable units
and an estimated \$2.5 million in annual property tax revenue.
After four years and
more than ten community meetings, Ald.\ Brian Hopkins announced in January 2025
that he would not advance the project, citing opposition from residents of
neighboring high-rises.
He was explicit about the source of his authority:
``Ultimately I am the decision maker on this.
I'm not going to hide from that.
This is a zoning decision and in Chicago we have something called aldermanic
prerogative.''
\citep{achong_shortened_2025} Within two weeks the developer
resubmitted a 349-unit proposal, cutting 151 units (30 percent) and 101 feet from the tower; Hopkins then approved it \citep{real_deal_old_town_2026}.
The episode captures both margins this paper studies: the approved
project is smaller than what the developer proposed (intensive margin), and the
four-year ordeal signals to other developers the cost of attempting large projects
in wards with active aldermanic gatekeeping (extensive margin).

\paragraph{Federal scrutiny.}
In 2023, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) concluded that aldermanic prerogative operates as a ``local veto'' that allows wards to block, deter, or downsize affordable housing, especially in majority-white areas \citep{chase_chicago_2023}; outside groups have made similar civil-rights arguments \citep{shriver_center_on_poverty_law_city_2019}.
The question of whether to reform or abolish aldermanic privilege remains a central issue in Chicago politics.\footnote{See
\url{https://news.wttw.com/elections/voters-guide/2023/races/city-council}, where
each city council candidate was asked specifically about their stance on aldermanic
prerogative.}

\subsection{Building Permits and the Permit Process}
\label{subsec:permit_pipeline}

The City's public data contain permits recorded by the Department of Buildings since 2006.
Each record reports when the application began and when the permit was issued, but cannot capture projects abandoned before they progress far enough to generate a permit record.
I use the universe of ``high-discretion'' permits from 2006--2022 that have valid geographic coordinates and nonnegative processing times.
\onlineappendixlink{A.3}{subsec:event_study_panel} describes the separate sample used to estimate stringency scores in the event study.
I divide permits into two groups based on how much aldermanic discretion they are likely to face.

\textit{High-discretion permits} are permits for projects where aldermen are most likely to matter: new construction, renovation or alteration, demolition, porch construction, and reinstated permits.\footnote{Porch construction permits have received extra scrutiny since the \href{https://chicago.suntimes.com/city-hall/2015/8/25/18590130/12-years-after-lincoln-park-porch-collapse-city-aims-to-speed-up-permits}{2003 balcony collapse}.}
These are the permit types most likely to draw community opposition and face delays due to community meetings or direct aldermanic intervention.
\textit{Low-discretion permits} are all remaining permit types outside this high-discretion group, excluding signs.
I use them as a placebo comparison group because they are generally less exposed to discretionary aldermanic intervention.

High-discretion permits take longer to process and vary more across aldermen: their mean processing time is 57.3 days, compared with 26.2 days for low-discretion permits (Figure~\ref{fig:permit_high_low_processing}).
Processing time is negatively associated with ward-month permit volume for high-discretion permits but weakly positively associated for low-discretion permits, consistent with the former capturing regulatory friction rather than general administrative capacity.
\onlineappendixlink{Table C.3}{tab:permit_summary_stats} reports the corresponding statistics.

\subsection{Regulatory Stringency Scores}
\label{subsec:stringency}

I construct the stringency score using permit records from 2006--2022.
I first adjust processing times for observed differences across wards, locations, permits, and months, then estimate the remaining alderman-specific differences.
The first stage regresses log processing time at the permit level on ward-level demographic controls (median household income, racial composition, homeownership rate, population), permit-location geographic controls (distance to the central business district (CBD), lakefront proximity, Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) rail access), lagged ward-month permit volume, and fixed effects for year-month, permit type, and review type:
%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stringency_reg1}
    \log(\text{Processing Time}_{i}) = X_i'\theta + \phi_{m(i)} + \psi_{c(i)} + \xi_{v(i)} + \varepsilon_{i},
\end{equation}
%
where $i$ indexes permits, $X_i$ contains the observed permit and location controls, and $m(i)$, $c(i)$, and $v(i)$ denote the permit's year-month, permit class, and review type.
\onlineappendixlink{Table B.1}{tab:stage1_residualization} reports the coefficients on the first-stage controls.

The second stage averages the adjusted processing times by ward and month, then regresses those averages on alderman indicators and the same lagged ward-month permit-volume control.
Ward-months are weighted by the number of permits observed:
%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stringency_reg2}
    \bar{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{wm} = \mu + \alpha_{a(w,m)} + \chi \text{Volume}_{w,m-1} + \nu_{wm}.
\end{equation}
%
Here $a(w,m)$ denotes the alderman serving ward $w$ in month $m$.
The raw alderman fixed effects $\hat{\alpha}_a$ can be noisy for aldermen with few permits.
I therefore apply empirical Bayes shrinkage \citep{kline_systemic_2022, kline_discrimination_2024} to pull imprecise estimates toward the grand mean, then standardize the final scores, denoted $S_a$, to have mean zero and unit standard deviation.

\onlineappendixlink{Figure B.1}{fig:stringency_diagnostics} maps the final stringency scores in 2014 and 2022, shows their distribution, and reports a low within-ward correlation between predecessor and successor aldermen ($\rho \approx 0.16$).
The scores vary across wealthy and poor neighborhoods and across the North and South Sides, supporting an alderman-specific rather than ward-level interpretation.

\subsection{New Construction Data}
\label{subsec:new_construction_data}

I construct a citywide dataset of new residential construction in Chicago between 2006 and 2022 using publicly available data from the Cook County Assessor's Office.
I combine residential improvement characteristics with commercial valuation records for properties assessed under multifamily model groups.
Together, these files report dwelling units, stories, building area, and land area.
I locate each project using Assessor parcel records, assign it to a ward using City of Chicago shapefiles, and define the construction date as June 15 of the recorded year built since I cannot observe an exact date.
For the boundary-discontinuity analyses, I divide ward boundaries into approximately 1,320-foot segments and assign each project to the nearest segment.
Because some ward boundaries are several miles long, the segments ensure that compared projects are near both the same boundary and each other.
I keep projects with positive lot area, positive building area, and at least one dwelling unit.

Because zoning measured today may reflect subsequent changes, I assign each project the broad zoning group in force on its assigned construction date.
\onlineappendixlink{A.4}{subsec:new_construction_sample} describes the historical zoning reconstruction.

For each project I compute two standard density measures: Floor Area Ratio (FAR), defined as total building area divided by lot area, and Dwelling Units Per Acre (DUPAC), defined as dwelling units divided by lot acreage.
Distance to the nearest ward boundary is computed after projecting project locations and boundaries to the Illinois East State Plane coordinate system and is reported in feet.

Table~\ref{tab:summary_stats} presents summary statistics.
The estimation sample contains 3,692 new residential projects within 500~ft of a ward boundary, including 822 classified as multifamily.
I report the multifamily sample separately because aldermanic discretion is likely to matter most for larger projects.

\subsection{Rental Listings}
\label{subsec:rental_listings}

RentHub provides scraped rental listings.
I use Chicago listings from 2014 through December 2022 and collapse repeated scrapes to one floorplan-month observation, using the median asking rent observed that month.
Rents are expressed in 2022 dollars using the monthly Chicago CPI-U.

\subsection{Home Sales}
\label{subsec:home_sales}

Home sales come from Cook County Assessor records.
I use filtered transactions of single-family homes, townhouses, and small multifamily buildings (2--6 units) from 2006 through 2022.
Prices are expressed in 2022 dollars using the monthly Chicago CPI-U and are not winsorized.
Full 14-digit property index numbers (PINs) determine location and date-specific ward assignments, and same-year Assessor records provide property characteristics.
I exclude transactions whose exact day is unavailable when an adjoining ward changes aldermen or the ward map changes during the reported month.

\onlineappendixlink{A}{app:data_sources} provides additional detail on the rental and home sales data.
