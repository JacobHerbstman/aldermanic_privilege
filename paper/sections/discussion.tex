\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:interpretation_mechanisms}

Taken together, the redistricting and boundary results show that individual politicians create meaningfully different regulatory environments within a single city.
Within-census-block variation shows that permitting falls when a census block is reassigned to a more stringent alderman, while completed projects on the more-stringent side of ward boundaries are built at lower density.
The price and rent comparisons are more tentative, but their direction is consistent with these differences in housing supply.

The central implication of these results is that the effect of political fragmentation on housing supply extends beyond formal zoning rules.
Two otherwise similar projects governed by the same municipal code can face different timelines, negotiations, and prospects for approval because they are represented by different aldermen.
Political fragmentation can therefore arise within a city, even without separate municipal governments or formally different regulatory codes.

These informal regulations can affect development along both extensive and intensive margins.
Additional meetings, redesigns, and uncertain approval timelines reduce the expected return to development.
Developers may respond to these additional demands, or the threat of them, by shrinking or abandoning projects before they enter the permitting data.
The Fern Hill episode described in Section~\ref{sec:data} illustrates these margins.
The project that ultimately received approval was smaller than the original proposal, and approval followed four years of meetings and negotiation.
The time-to-build framework in Section~\ref{sec:model} captures the underlying economic force: delay lowers the value of development, reduces the scale of projects that remain viable, and can cause marginal projects to disappear.

These findings complement \citet{khan_decentralized_2021}, who shows that Chicago aldermen approve smaller increases in allowed density near ward boundaries, where their constituents capture fewer of the benefits from development.
Informal political control can therefore affect not only what the zoning code allows, but also whether development proceeds and what ultimately gets built.

\subsection{Informal Discretion Beyond Chicago}
\label{subsec:beyond_chicago}

Chicago provides an especially clear setting because aldermanic privilege is unusually powerful, but the underlying institution is not unique to Chicago.
Local council-member deference in land-use decisions has also been documented in Philadelphia, Los Angeles, and New York City
\citep{the_pew_charitable_trusts_philadelphias_2015, ryu_limiting_2020, anbinder_new_2025, new_york_city_charter_revision_commission_charter_2025, mitre-becerril_does_2024, burnett_local_2014, jacobson_dysfunctional_2023}.

In many major U.S. cities, individual politicians can create distinct regulatory environments when projects require discretionary approval and other officials defer to the representative of the affected district.
Under these conditions, the identity and preferences of the local politician can matter even when the written rules remain similar across districts.
The broader lesson---that informal regulations can dampen housing supply and affect affordability---likely applies to other major cities as well.

\subsection{Policy Implications}
\label{subsec:policy_implications}

Local politicians may possess useful information about neighborhood conditions and provide residents with a clear channel for political accountability.
But ward-level representatives also have little reason to internalize the citywide benefits of additional housing, and informal discretion allows them to impose delay and uncertainty after the formal rules have been established.
The resulting regulatory environment may reflect the preferences of an individual officeholder or a narrow slice of constituents rather than a broad city welfare metric.

While this paper cannot pin down the optimal balance between local input and centralized authority, it does point to predictability and transparency as important goals for reform.
Expanding the scope of by-right approval would allow projects that comply with established rules to proceed without obtaining the support of an individual alderman.
Published approval criteria, enforceable timelines, and meaningful appeal procedures could preserve local participation while limiting uncertainty.
Moving some decisions to citywide bodies could also make it easier to account for housing benefits that extend beyond the ward in which a project is located.

Chicago's lack of a city charter makes the allocation of authority particularly dependent on unwritten norms.
A charter would not by itself eliminate aldermanic influence, but it could formally define approval authority, procedures, and avenues for appeal \citep{mcclintock_what_2025}.
More generally, reforms aimed only at zoning classifications or formal voting rules may have limited effects if individual politicians retain informal control over permitting, project design, or administrative implementation.

These institutional choices matter as Chicago confronts rapidly rising rents \citep{davis_chicagos_2026}.
More broadly, the results show why evaluations of land-use regulation should look beyond the written zoning code.
When individual political actors possess substantial discretionary authority, who administers the rules can matter as much as the rules themselves.
