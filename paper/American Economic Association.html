1 Introduction

Housing costs in the United States have risen sharply, and a large literature points to land
use regulation as a central cause. Restrictive zoning, permitting frictions, and inclusionary
zoning requirements limit housing supply, raise prices, and distort the allocation of labor
across cities (Glaeser, Gyourko and Saks, 2005; Saiz, 2010; Turner, Haughwout and Van
Der Klaauw, 2014; Hsieh and Moretti, 2019; Duranton and Puga, 2023; Soltas, 2024; Soltas
and Gruber, 2026). Formal rules, however, are only one part of the regulatory environment.
A small but growing body of work argues that the political environment and fragmentation
of regulatory authority matter as much as the formal regulations themselves (Mayer and
Somerville, 2000a; Hilber and Robert-Nicoud, 2013; Khan, 2021; Mast, 2024). When devel-
opers face long and unpredictable timelines, projects shrink, marginal investments become
unviable, and housing supply falls (Cunningham, 2006; Bloom, 2009; Oh, Yang and Yoon,
2024). Despite this preliminary evidence, there is little empirical work on how individual
politicians use these informal regulatory powers to shape housing supply.
This paper studies this informal regulatory power in the context of Chicago’s “aldermanic
privilege,” the unwritten but influential power of each of Chicago’s 50 city council members
(aldermen) to unilaterally block or approve development in their ward. Chicago has no
city charter, so there is no statutory basis for this power (McClintock, 2025). It operates
through an unspoken mutual understanding: other aldermen vote with the local member on
land use matters in exchange for similar support in their own wards, city agencies follow the
local alderman’s lead, and developers understand that major projects require the alderman’s
blessing (Thale, 2005; Einstein, Glick and Palmer, 2019; Khan, 2021). This creates sharp
changes in regulatory authority at ward boundaries, even when the neighborhood and zoning
are quite similar. Two parcels on opposite sides of the same street, in the same neighbor-
hood, with the same zoning, can face very different regulatory regimes depending on which
alderman they interact with. I use this sudden change in regulatory authority to study how
informal regulations affect housing supply and prices.

2
To measure the regulatory environment each alderman creates, I construct “regulatory
stringency scores” from processing times for permits likely to involve substantial aldermanic
discretion, which include new construction, renovations, and demolitions. I deem these
“high-discretion” permits since they are the most likely to require aldermanic approval. The
motivation for using processing time variation is simple: delay makes projects less valuable,
and a developer who expects years of meetings, redesigns, and uncertainty may build a
smaller project or be deterred from building at all (Kydland and Prescott, 1982; Mayer and
Somerville, 2000a; Paciorek, 2013; Gyourko, Hartley and Krimmel, 2021; Soltas and Gruber,
2026).
I first use the 2015 ward redistricting to test whether permitting changes when a census
block is reassigned to a different ward, which isolates the keeping location fixed. Using this
within-census-block variation, I find that reassignment toward greater stringency reduces
the annual number of high-discretion permits recorded by the city by about 12% in the five
years following the remap. This result is driven asymmetrically by movement toward more
stringent aldermen, and those census blocks see a 23% reduction in high-discretion permits
relative to a 6.5% rise in permits for those who move toward a more lenient alderman.
A relatively precise null result for low-discretion permits shows that these effects operate
through discretionary authority rather than general ward-level differences in bureaucratic
efficiency.
I then look at downstream housing completions to estimate the effects of this informal
regulatory discretion on realized housing supply. I employ nonparametric regression discon-
tinuity designs at ward boundaries following Bayer, Ferreira and McMillan (2007); Kulka,
Sood and Chiumenti (2026) to estimate effects on new construction density and find a 12%
reduction in FAR and a 16% reduction in DUPAC on the more-stringent side of ward bound-
aries, with slightly larger effects for multifamily new construction.
I finally examine whether housing costs are higher in the places where the supply evidence
predicts they should be. I find a sharp increase in home prices across the boundary of about


3
4.6%. I also find a 1.7% increase in listed rents, but the estimate is imprecise and not
statistically significant. While these results are more suggestive, they are consistent with
lower supply on the more stringent side of ward boundaries and suggest that the uncertainty
induced by informal regulations and political actors can have direct effects on affordability.
This paper contributes most directly to work on local political control and fragmented
land-use authority. Fischel (2009) argues that homeowners use local institutions to restrict
development and protect property values, and subsequent work shows how decentralized
jurisdictions can adopt restrictions that are locally rational but collectively costly (Hilber and
Robert-Nicoud, 2013; Ortalo-Magné and Prat, 2014; Monarrez and Schönholzer, 2023; Mast,
2024; Bordeu, 2025; Kulka, Sood and Chiumenti, 2026). In Chicago, Khan (2021) finds that
aldermen approve smaller rezonings near ward boundaries, while Freemark (2020) studies
formal upzoning. I show that a similar coordination problem operates among representatives
within one city and affects permit activity and completed construction, not only formal
zoning decisions.
The paper also contributes to work on permitting delays and time-to-build. Development
lags are a first-order determinant of housing supply (Mayer and Somerville, 2000a,b; Paciorek,
2013). Recent work finds that rules-based planning shortens construction timelines and that
developers place substantial value on lots with ready-to-issue permits (Lyons and Sweeney,
2025; Soltas and Gruber, 2026). I identify individual officials as a source of delay and connect
their discretion to both permit activity and completed housing density.



2 Institutional Background and Data

2.1 Aldermanic Privilege and the Development Process

Chicago’s City Council consists of 50 aldermen, each representing a single ward. Chicago’s
uniquely informal norms give each alderman broad authority over development within ward
boundaries. This practice, known as “aldermanic privilege” or “aldermanic prerogative,”


4
rests on custom rather than formal law, in part because Chicago has no city charter (Mc-
Clintock, 2025). On matters affecting a single ward, other aldermen defer to the local
representative, and city agencies follow the alderman’s lead (Thale, 2005; Zhang, 2011). The
convention is strong enough that aldermen have often been described as “little mayors” gov-
erning their wards as personal fiefdoms (Einhorn, 1991). One alderman described the system
candidly: “I often liken the City of Chicago to a feudal system, where the mayor is sort of
a de facto king. And each alderman is the lord of their individual fiefdom.” (Keefe, 2013)


The formal mechanism. Two reforms narrowed this authority in 2019. A new construc-
tion code repealed a rule that had generally required the Department of Buildings to notify
the local alderman of permit applications and wait ten days. Mayor Lightfoot’s Executive
Order 2019-2 separately directed city departments not to defer to aldermen when municipal
law did not require it. Neither reform eliminated aldermanic influence: other notification
requirements remained, departments continued to solicit aldermanic input, and aldermen
retained City Council authority over zoning and other legislative approvals (City of Chicago,
2019; Mayor’s Press Office, 2019; Schutz, 2019). Lightfoot captured the problem directly: “If
you’ve got 50 separate fiefdoms that you have to deal with on issues like affordable housing,
or just getting a sign on your building, it makes doing business with the city of Chicago
damn near impossible.” (Spielman, 2019b) The Metropolitan Planning Council made the
same point from the developer side: “before a development even gets to the point of being
indefinitely delayed, many never make it past ‘Go’ because of required aldermanic letters of
support.” (Metropolitan Planning Council, 2019)


How prerogative shapes development. Aldermanic discretion enters well before formal
permit issuance and continues after a project is filed.
Pre-application. Experienced Chicago developers know that any project requiring rezon-
ing, or smaller project modifications, will require obtaining the alderman’s support before
formal papers are filed. While not a legal requirement, it is all but required in practice:

5
without the alderman’s support, a developer risks investing heavily in design, legal, and
engineering work before facing rejection at the political stage. One anonymous Chicago
architect described the dynamic: “To get the alderman, you have to get the neighborhood
group, and that’s where the trouble comes in. It’s fraught with risk, even to have this
conversation.” (Giangreco, 2024)
Rezoning. For projects requiring a change to the zoning code, the alderman’s role is
effectively dispositive. Although zoning amendments formally require a City Council vote,
the council “routinely shows unquestioning deference to local aldermen even in the absence of
any articulated local concern” (Chase, 2023). Equally consequential is the alderman’s ability
to preemptively downzone parcels to foreclose future development. Joseph Schwieterman, a
DePaul University professor who studies Chicago land use, noted that the practice “creates
real uncertainty for real estate developers when downzoning becomes ad hoc or driven by the
desire of an alderman to settle scores.” (Gallun, 2019) Documented episodes include Ald.
Brendan Reilly’s proposals to downzone a River North site where a developer had proposed
a 60-story tower and a separate site to block a 615-room hotel, and a Pilsen downzoning
orchestrated by Ald. Solis that stripped a parcel of its residential designation to kill a planned
465-unit apartment project.
Permit processing. For projects that proceed by right under existing zoning, the alderman
still retains substantial informal influence over the Department of Buildings permit queue.
The corruption case against Ald. Ed Burke illustrated the mechanism in an extreme form:
federal prosecutors documented how his staff withheld routine permits–including a driveway
permit for a fast-food restaurant–until the property owner directed legal business to Burke’s
law firm (Spielman, 2019a). The case made visible what developers had long understood:
in Chicago, permit timing is another lever aldermen can use to delay and suppress projects
they oppose. Interviews I have conducted with a former alderman detailed substantial use
of the permit process to deter developers from trying to overrule the alderman on rezoning
and planning matters earlier in the process.


6
A recent example. Developer Fern Hill began community outreach in 2021, proposing
a roughly 500-unit mixed-income tower at a transit-accessible site in the 2nd Ward, with
approximately 100 affordable units and an estimated $2.5 million in annual property tax
revenue. After four years and more than ten community meetings, Ald. Brian Hopkins an-
nounced in January 2025 that he would not advance the project, citing opposition from
residents of neighboring high-rises. He was explicit about the source of his authority: “Ulti-
mately I am the decision maker on this. I’m not going to hide from that. This is a zoning
decision and in Chicago we have something called aldermanic prerogative.” (Achong, 2025)
Within two weeks the developer resubmitted a 349-unit proposal, cutting 151 units (30 per-
cent) and 101 feet from the tower; Hopkins then approved it (TRD Staff, 2026). The episode
captures both margins this paper studies: the approved project is smaller than what the
developer proposed (intensive margin), and the four-year ordeal signals to other developers
the cost of attempting large projects in wards with active aldermanic gatekeeping (extensive
margin).


Federal scrutiny. In 2023, HUD concluded that aldermanic prerogative operates as a
“local veto” that allows wards to block, deter, or downsize affordable housing, especially
in majority-white areas (Chase, 2023); outside groups have made similar civil-rights argu-
ments (Shriver Center on Poverty Law, 2019). The question of whether to reform or abolish
aldermanic privilege remains a central issue in Chicago politics.1

2.4 New Construction Data

I construct a citywide dataset of new residential construction in Chicago between 2006 and
2022 using publicly available data from the Cook County Assessor’s Office. I combine residen-
tial improvement characteristics with commercial valuation records for properties assessed
under multifamily model groups. Together, these files report dwelling units, stories, build-

9
ing area, and land area. I locate each project using Assessor parcel records, assign it to a
ward using City of Chicago shapefiles, and define the construction date as June 15 of the
recorded year built since I cannot observe an exact date. I keep projects with positive lot
area, positive building area, and at least one dwelling unit.
Formal zoning is an important control, but zoning measured today may reflect changes
made for the project itself. I therefore assign each project in the boundary samples to
the broad zoning group in force on June 15 of its construction year. I reconstruct the
zoning history from official City zoning maps from 2012, 2014, and 2016 and passed map
amendments dating back to 2006. Annual Cook County parcel maps allow me to follow
sites whose parcel identifiers changed over time, and I retain planned developments as a
separate zoning group. The reconstruction covers every project used in the main and placebo
boundary analyses and reproduces the classifications observed in later official zoning maps.
Online Appendix A.4 provides additional detail.
For each project I compute two standard density measures: Floor Area Ratio (FAR),
defined as total building area divided by lot area, and Dwelling Units Per Acre (DUPAC),
defined as dwelling units divided by lot acreage. Distance to the nearest ward boundary is
computed after projecting project locations and boundaries to the Illinois East State Plane
coordinate system and is reported in feet.
Table 1presents summary statistics. The estimation sample contains 3,692 new resi-
dential projects within 500ft of a ward boundary, including 822 classified as multifamily. I
report the multifamily sample separately because aldermanic discretion is likely to matter
most for larger projects. I focus on the multifamily sample and report the all-construction
estimates for comparison.

3.3 Interpretation of the Stringency Score

In the model, these frictions enter through the index rT (s). A higher value means revenue
arrives later and is worth less today. That affects both the developer’s intensive-margin
density choice and the extensive-margin decision to build or not.
The aldermanic stringency score I construct in Section 2.3 is an empirical proxy for
T (s). It captures alderman-specific differences in permit processing times after adjusting
for observed ward characteristics. Other dimensions of regulatory friction, such as impact
fees, design mandates, or community benefit requirements, would enter as additional costs
in C(·) and reinforce the same qualitative predictions. The core idea is that longer observed
processing times for permits on larger discretionary projects are a noisy but informative
signal of delay that lowers density, reduces aggregate supply, and may raise prices.



4 Results

4.1 Permit Activity After Redistricting

I first ask whether permit activity changes when the same location is assigned to a different
alderman. Chicago’s 2015 ward redistricting moved some census blocks toward wards rep-
resented by more stringent aldermen and others toward wards represented by more lenient
aldermen. Nearby blocks that remained in their original ward provide the comparison group.
The design therefore holds the physical location fixed while changing its assigned regulatory
environment. The primary analysis sample keeps all boundaries where the aldermen repre-
senting both the origin and destination wards in 2014 remained in office when the new map
took effect, since newly elected aldermen could have preferences toward development that
are endogenous to the previous development activity in the ward. I also require each block to
have at least one high-discretion permit during the five years before redistricting. Without
that restriction, any later permit on a block with no previous activity would mechanically

5 Discussion

5.1 Interpretation of Estimates

Together, the boundary and redistricting results show lower completed density and fewer
recorded permits under more stringent aldermen. The rent and sale-price comparisons sug-
gest that these supply differences may also be reflected in local housing costs, an important
concern as Chicago rents continue to rise rapidly, outpacing much of the country (Davis,


17
2026). Aldermanic privilege creates sharp changes in the regulatory environment at ward
boundaries. The uncertainty and inconsistency this creates make the development process
slower and less predictable, and likely deters some projects from ever breaking ground.
Khan (2021) studies project size among developments seeking a rezoning. Developers
receive smaller increases in allowed density near ward boundaries where aldermen cannot
internalize the benefits of development. My permitting and density estimates extend this
result to realized development: completed buildings are less dense under more stringent
aldermen.
The Fern Hill episode described in Section 2illustrates why this margin may matter.
The approved project was smaller than originally proposed, while four years of meetings and
negotiation show the costs that may cause other developers to walk away before filing. The
time-to-build framework in Section 3formalizes this idea: additional delay reduces the scale
of projects that proceed and can make marginal projects unviable. While I cannot observe
projects that were deterred before coming up for a vote in the city council, my permit and
density results show that the projects that do proceed are less dense under more stringent
aldermen, which is consistent with the idea that some projects are deterred entirely.


5.2 Policy Implications

These results speak to current policy proposals to limit aldermanic influence in land use.
They suggest that reform must address both formal approval authority and the informal
influence aldermen exercise over permitting and project design.
The results also show how informal rules allow individual political actors to shape citywide
housing policy. Although aldermanic privilege is unusually powerful in Chicago, the practice
of deferring to the local council member on land-use decisions appears in other large cities,
including New York, Philadelphia, and Los Angeles (The Pew Charitable Trusts, 2015; Ryu,
2020; Anbinder, 2025; New York City Charter Revision Commission, 2025). Similar forms
of council-member deference in other cities suggest that the mechanism may extend beyond


18
Chicago.
Centralizing land use authority by shifting rezoning and permitting authority from in-
dividual aldermen to citywide bodies would let decision-makers internalize the full benefits
of housing production, though at the cost of sacrificing some local knowledge. Expanding
by-right development so that code-compliant projects proceed automatically without alder-
manic review would remove the discretionary layer while preserving community preferences
as expressed in the zoning code. But as Section 2discusses, aldermen retain unilateral power
to downzone parcels preemptively, which means they can often undo by-right protections be-
fore a developer applies. Another oft-discussed reform is the creation of a city charter, which
would allow all rules to be codified, which could limit discretionary power relative to the
current system of unwritten norms and traditions (McClintock, 2025). Reform likely needs
to address both statutory and informal channels to be successful.



6 Conclusion

This paper studies how informal regulations and the discretion of individual political actors
can influence housing supply within a city. I focus on Chicago’s aldermanic privilege, the
informal power of each of the city’s 50 aldermen to unilaterally block or approve development
in their ward, and show that it has measurable effects on development activity in the city.
Using permit processing times for projects where aldermen are most likely to matter,
I construct aldermanic stringency scores that isolate the alderman-specific component of
regulatory delay after removing variation attributable to ward characteristics. Using 2015
ward redistricting as identifying variation, I find that reassignment toward greater stringency
reduces the annual number of high-discretion permits recorded by the city by about 12% in
the five years following the remap. A boundary comparison shows that new construction is
less dense on the more stringent side of ward boundaries, with multifamily FAR about 14%
lower and DUPAC about 18% lower immediately across the boundary. Home sales see a large



19
4.6% increase in prices immediately across the boundary, and listed rents see a suggestive
increase of about 1.7%, although the estimate is imprecise. The supply-side designs provide
causal evidence that variation in informal discretionary authority, beyond the formal rules
on the books, affects housing production. My results suggest that reforming the land-use
policy in Chicago could affect housing production, but that effective reform would require
addressing the power of individual discretion in the process along with the formal rules that
have been the subject of most prior research.
