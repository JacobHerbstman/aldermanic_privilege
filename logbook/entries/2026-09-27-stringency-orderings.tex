\section*{Which side is stricter: the main estimates under other stringency measures}
\textit{September 27, 2026. Agent-drafted exploratory checks requested by Jacob, on the current permit-based
construction data and joint-service specification; no production or manuscript result was changed.}

The boundary designs use only which alderman of a facing pair is stricter, so the relevant agreement between two
stringency measures is the share of facing pairs they order the same way. Re-estimating the 2006--2022 index with
different estimation choices keeps most orderings: without shrinkage, as a one-stage mean residual, with an
unweighted second stage, or without the volume control, 91 to 99 percent of facing multifamily pairs keep their
order. Without the permit- and review-type fixed effects, or in days rather than log days, agreement weighted by
buildings falls to about 80 percent. Developer, real-estate and land-use-professional donation shares order facing
pairs the same way as the index only 35 to 47 percent of the time, so donations provide no validation of the index.

The two main results respond differently. The pooled permit effect of $-0.127$ stays between $-0.09$ and $-0.18$
under every alternative ordering except the index without the volume control ($-0.067$), and is $-0.181$ when the
reassigned blocks that any estimation variant classifies differently are dropped. The multifamily density average of
$-0.061$ survives the three smallest changes ($-0.059$ to $-0.069$) but is $-0.017$ to $-0.043$ under the other
three, slightly positive when pairs are ordered by raw processing times, and $-0.033$ (0.034) when the third of
buildings on boundaries some variant flips are dropped. All-construction density stays near zero throughout.

The multifamily estimate is not carried by one boundary: leaving out one ward pair at a time keeps it between
$-0.077$ and $-0.054$. It is carried by the boundaries whose ordering is least certain. On the 695 buildings where at
least one variant disagrees (median index gap 0.22 SD) the average difference is $-0.091$ (0.060); on the 1,456
where every variant agrees (median gap 0.98 SD) it is $-0.033$ (0.034). It shows no dose response: by index gap it is
$-0.093$, $-0.126$, $-0.011$ and $-0.053$ for gaps of 0--0.25, 0.25--0.5, 0.5--1 and over 1 SD, and a linear
interaction with the gap is $+0.007$ (0.046). By the raw mean log processing-time gap it is largest where the index's
stricter alderman is faster in raw terms ($-0.12$ to $-0.13$, 656 buildings) and smallest where that alderman is
slower ($-0.03$ to $-0.05$, 1,495 buildings).

Raw processing times and the index agree much more closely in the event study than at the density boundaries. For
reassigned blocks, the index's stricter alderman is slower in raw days for 76 percent of blocks, with a median gap of
5.0 days, and the index and raw log gaps correlate at 0.64. For multifamily buildings the figures are 59 percent, 1.2
days and 0.22, so the density ordering rests mostly on the index's adjustments. Across aldermen, the score change
without the type fixed effects correlates 0.33 with the share of demolition permits and 0.18 with the share of
standard-plan reviews; the change in days correlates 0.25 with the share of permits taking over 180 days and 0.33
with the demolition share.

These results replace the ordering diagnostics of September 14--15, which used the earlier Assessor-based
construction data and a different specification; the qualitative conclusion is the same, that the multifamily
density estimate is more sensitive to the ordering than the permit estimate. The absence of a gap gradient is new.
The agent's reading is that the density difference should not be presented as evidence that stricter aldermen, as
measured, build less densely; how to describe it is Jacob's decision.
