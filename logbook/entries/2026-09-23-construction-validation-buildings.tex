\section*{How accurate is the permit construction data, and what is one row?}
\textit{September 23, 2026. Agent-drafted data review; the unit-of-observation choice is Jacob's.}

A stratified random sample of 225 cases from the permit-based construction data (dataset at commit
\texttt{9736e3c8}; \texttt{tasks/audits/construction\_hand\_checks}) was reviewed case by case against permit text
and Assessor histories by agent reviewers, with the main claims checked against the data. Weighted by stratum
population, 90.7 percent of density-eligible buildings were correct. Buildings measured on the permit's own parcels,
three quarters of the sample, were right in 24 of 25 cases, as were lot links; the weakest group was the
Assessor-only 2006--2007 buildings (17 of 25). Among withheld cases, 18 of 25 ``no permit found'' buildings were
genuine new construction, 14 of them with a permit that should have linked; 21 of 25 unit disagreements were
recoverable; and of permits that reached no building, 13 of 25 were never built, 5 were tax-exempt housing without
Assessor characteristics, and only 2 were missed links.

One mechanism explained the largest error in four strata: when a condominium declaration or subdivision retires a
parcel number, the building reappears on new parcels. The permit kept its parent lot's record while the finished
homes sat unlinked, or an Assessor-only parent card duplicated its children. On the Cleveland--Hobbie--Oak site,
eight permits for rowhome rows and two condominium buildings all listed one parent lot; the Assessor recorded a
39-unit card on it in 2014 and about 48 child parcels from 2016. The paper's data counts this site 40 times (the
parent card and 39 homes); ours had counted it zero times in the density sample. Jacob chose one row per building.
Each permit's parcels now include the successors of its listed parcels (each new parcel descends from the nearest
retired parcel on its block), a record reached by several permits belongs to the permit at its address, and
hand-named lots and homes join their permit's parcels. That site is now eight building rows; six match their
permits' dwelling counts exactly, and two (449 W Hobbie, 1001 N Cleveland) reach only one of their homes and are
withheld. Allowing several parents per new parcel recovered one more row but broke others (94 fewer eligible
buildings) and was not adopted. Two hand decisions that had assigned a whole divided site to one permit were retired.

The file now has 18,051 rows (18,875 before) and 14,053 density-eligible buildings (14,033): 11,776 permitted
(11,564) and 2,277 Assessor-only (2,469; the difference is mostly parent cards removed as duplicates). Counted as
the paper does, by completion year 2006--2022, there are 13,022 eligible buildings against the paper's roughly
13,600, part of which is the paper's double counting of divided sites. ``No permit found'' buildings fell from
1,776 to 1,077; flagged measured buildings rose from 842 to 959, mostly divided sites whose successors include a
neighbor built without its own permit.

\textit{Update, same day.} A second review round on the rebuilt data (commit \texttt{667f19d4}, a new stratified
sample of 225) found 97.5 percent of eligible buildings correct, weighted by stratum population (90.7 before).
Buildings on the permit's own parcels, parcel successors and added homes were 25 of 25 each; Assessor-only
2006--2007 buildings rose to 22 of 25, the round-one duplicates gone. The weakest groups are small: floor-area
rebuilds (19 of 25: a wrong listed parcel, two repairs of existing buildings classified as new construction, two
transient measurement years) and lot or hand links (21 of 25). Among withheld cases, 14 of 25 ``no permit found''
buildings still have a permit that should link (new buildings filed as renovations, successors not traced, permits
listing an address range without parcels); 10 of 25 unit disagreements combine twin buildings on one Assessor record,
which cannot be split. Buildings are now measured on the record they hold most often in their first three assessment
years rather than the first year alone, keeping the first year's proration: 261 floor areas and 52 unit counts
changed, 69 buildings became eligible and 4 were withheld. The file has 14,129 eligible buildings (13,093 by
completion year 2006--2022) and 914 flagged measured buildings (959).

\textit{Second update.} Two more rules link withheld buildings to permits: about 30 new buildings filed as renovation,
easy or express permits (a first sentence that erects a new building of stated height with dwellings, and no work on
an existing one; a looser version admitted about 400 dormers, porches and build-outs and was rejected), and house
numbers a multi-dwelling permit lists on its street (``329, 335, 337, 339 EAST 25TH PLACE''; used by single-house
permits these named neighbors and took their homes, so they are not). Land below 100 square feet is now treated as a
recording error (one 73-unit building recorded on 1 square foot had put the multifamily mean FAR at 12.7; it is now
2.0, median 1.65). A building-by-building comparison with the previous version finds four eligible buildings lost:
that one, two single homes now correctly part of 7- and 8-home townhouse permits whose other homes were not found, and
one 6-unit condominium that a neighboring permit takes by address and then discards; two reorderings of the rules
that would keep it lost 7 and 81 buildings and were not adopted. A third review round (commit \texttt{3b438336}, a
new sample) found 96.5 percent of eligible buildings correct (97.5 in round two, within sampling error): permit
parcels, successors and added homes 25 of 25 again, lot and hand links 24, Assessor-only 20, floor-area rebuilds 20.
The file has 14,143 eligible buildings (11,846 permitted, 2,297 Assessor-only, 4,937 multifamily), 13,100 by
completion year 2006--2022, 1,042 ``no permit found'' buildings and 909 flagged. No regressions have been run on these
data.

\textit{Third update.} Assessor-only townhouses are now counted as permitted ones are: class 295 homes on consecutive
parcel numbers of a block (or alternating with garage parcels), first assessed in the same year with the same year
built, on the same side of the same street, are one building. Before, each of 877 such homes was a row, while a
townhouse permit is one row; 58 percent of Assessor-only single-family 2006--2007 buildings were townhouse homes,
against 6 percent of permitted ones. The rows are not multifamily, as permitted townhouse rows are not, and their
density is unchanged because the homes' floor area and land are summed. A permit erecting eight townhouses ``an
addition to'' four existing ones now counts as new construction (three permits gained, removing two fragments). A
fourth review round (dataset at commit \texttt{2ad05afb}) covered 50 random Assessor-only buildings and two groups
flagged by candidate precision rules, reviewers not told which case came from which group. Of 15 buildings at an
address with a permit for work on an existing building by the reported year built and no new-construction or wrecking
permit, 3 were correct and 10 were condominium conversions or rehabs given a new year built; of 12 first assessed five
or more years after the reported year built, 1 was correct (three were real buildings finished around 2012 under
permits, six could not be dated). Both are now flags that withhold density (57, 206 and 2 formerly eligible buildings).
The 50 unflagged buildings were 38 correct, 2 unsure, 3 not new, 4 mismeasured and 3 fragments, two of them of one
Monroe Street row now grouped. Assessor-only buildings remain the least accurate group (about 80 percent correct across rounds, against 96.5
for the data as a whole in round three). The file has 13,391 eligible buildings (11,849 permitted, 1,542
Assessor-only, 4,798 multifamily), 12,347 by completion year 2006--2022; a building-by-building comparison with the
previous version finds no eligible building lost except those the two new flags withhold.
